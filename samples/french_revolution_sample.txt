Chapter 3: The French Revolution (1789-1799)

3.1 Introduction
The French Revolution was a period of radical political and societal change in France that began with the Estates General of 1789 and ended with the formation of the French Consulate in November 1799. It fundamentally altered the course of modern history, triggering the decline of absolute monarchies and replacing them with republics and liberal democracies.

3.2 Causes of the French Revolution

Political Causes:
France was ruled by an absolute monarchy under King Louis XVI. The king had unlimited power and the common people had no say in governance. The Estates General had not been called since 1614.

Social Causes:
French society was divided into three estates:
- First Estate: Clergy (owned 10% of land, paid no taxes)
- Second Estate: Nobility (owned 25% of land, paid almost no taxes)
- Third Estate: Common people including peasants, bourgeoisie, and urban workers (97% of population, paid all taxes)

This inequality created deep resentment among the Third Estate.

Economic Causes:
- France was bankrupt due to expensive wars (including support for American Revolution)
- Poor harvests led to bread shortages and rising prices
- Heavy taxation on the poor while the rich were exempt
- The government spent lavishly on the Palace of Versailles

Intellectual Causes:
Enlightenment thinkers challenged traditional authority:
- John Locke: natural rights (life, liberty, property)
- Jean-Jacques Rousseau: social contract theory
- Voltaire: freedom of speech and religion
- Montesquieu: separation of powers

3.3 Key Events

The Storming of the Bastille (July 14, 1789):
A mob attacked the Bastille fortress in Paris, a symbol of royal authority. This event marked the beginning of the revolution and July 14 is now France's national day.

The Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen (August 1789):
This document proclaimed that all men are born free and equal in rights. Key rights included liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression. It was inspired by the American Declaration of Independence.

The Women's March on Versailles (October 1789):
Thousands of women marched to Versailles demanding bread. They forced the royal family to move to Paris, symbolizing the power of the people.

The Reign of Terror (1793-1794):
Led by Maximilien Robespierre and the Committee of Public Safety, thousands of perceived enemies of the revolution were executed by guillotine. An estimated 16,000-40,000 people were killed during this period.

3.4 Important Concepts

Sovereignty: The idea that ultimate political authority rests with the people, not the king.

Democracy: A system of government where power is held by the people through elected representatives.

Republic: A form of government where the country is considered a public matter, not the private property of rulers.

Nationalism: A sense of identity and loyalty to one's nation, which grew strongly during the revolution.

3.5 Impact and Legacy
- End of feudalism and absolute monarchy in France
- Spread of democratic ideals across Europe
- Inspired revolutions in Latin America, Haiti, and other regions
- The Napoleonic Code influenced legal systems worldwide
- Established the principle that government derives authority from citizens
- Led to the rise of Napoleon Bonaparte

3.6 Summary
The French Revolution transformed France from an absolute monarchy to a republic. It was caused by social inequality, economic crisis, and Enlightenment ideas. Key events include the Storming of the Bastille, the Declaration of Rights, and the Reign of Terror. Its legacy includes the spread of democracy and human rights worldwide.

Prerequisites: Basic understanding of European history, medieval feudal system, and the concept of monarchy.
